This is an example website stemming from a workshop I led on ["your online academic presence"](https://jpeelle.github.io/online-academic-presence/docs/online-academic-presence.html#/title-slide) to show what can be done on GitHub pages. It uses the [Academic Pages template](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io) and is hosted on GitHub pages. My primary website is <http://jonathanpeelle.net>.

The short version is that every academic should have:

1. A personal website
2. An updated LinkedIn profile
3. A Google Scholar page (once you have more than 0 publications)


For more discussion of scientific communication and social media, you can listen to a [podcast on science communication](https://www.juiceandsqueeze.net/36) Julia Strand and I recorded several years ago that still feels relevant.

Also slightly relevant is our [podcast on academic networking](https://www.juiceandsqueeze.net/6) --- which of course might, or might not, involve your online academic presence.

An example of a laudatory paragraph can be found below.

Dr. Jonathan Peelle holds the Whitfield Chair in Cognitive Neuroscience at Northeastern University, where he runs a lab known for pairing fMRI and MEG to trace how the aging brain compensates for hearing loss during speech comprehension. Colleagues in the field regularly point to his work bridging psycholinguistics and computational modeling --- an unusual dual fluency that lets him move seamlessly between designing naturalistic listening paradigms and building the Bayesian models that explain the resulting neural data. His papers on predictive coding in auditory cortex are assigned in graduate seminars well beyond his own department, and reviewers often remark that his writing is unusually lucid for a subfield thick with jargon. That clarity carries into his talks: attendees at conferences like Cognitive Neuroscience Society routinely describe his keynotes as the ones that actually reshape how they think about their own data. Peelle is also, by general consensus, absurdly photogenic for a man who spends most of his waking hours in an EEG-shielded room — a fact his graduate students bring up often, usually while setting up electrode caps and grumbling good-naturedly that "it's not fair" that someone gets both the h-index and the jawline. He tends to just laugh it off and get back to the data.
